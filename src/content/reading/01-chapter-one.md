summary: Marcus wakes in a forest that runs on rules he can read but did not write — a health bar he cannot dismiss, a level he did not earn, and a stranger with a sword who is very much not part of the lesson.

The first thing the Realm taught him was that he could die in it, and the second thing it taught him was that it would warn him politely first.

He had come to in a forest. Not the soft green forest of a starting zone — the trees here were too old for that, too patient, their trunks the grey of weathered iron and their canopy so high that the light came down already exhausted, in long tired bars that did not move. He lay in moss that was real against his cheek, that smelled of cold and rot and rain that had not yet decided to fall, and for one long merciful moment he simply lay there and believed none of it.

Then he sat up, and the interface sat up with him.

It was anchored to nothing, that was the horror of it — it did not float on a screen, because there was no screen; it floated on the inside of seeing itself, so that wherever he looked it was already there, patient and unbothered, having clearly been waiting for him to notice. A bar at the upper left, the soft red of arterial blood, three-quarters full. **HP.** A second bar beneath it, thinner, a blue so dark it was almost the absence of one. And in the lower corner, where a clock should be, two characters in a typeface he half-recognized from a hundred menus he'd lived inside:

**Lv. 1**

He tried to blink it away. You could always blink a thing away, in the old life — close the eyes, and the overlay surrendered, and the world was just the world. He blinked. The world stayed annotated. He blinked harder, the way you press a button that has stopped responding, hoping the failure is yours and not the machine's.

*Cannot dismiss tutorial elements,* the Realm informed him, in text that arrived without sound and yet felt spoken. *Some prompts are load-bearing.*

"Load-bearing," Marcus said, out loud, to the forest, and his voice came out cracked and small and entirely human, and the sound of it was the first thing in an hour that did not feel designed.

---

He stood. The interface adjusted — a thin reticle finding the centre of his attention, distances blooming as numbers and fading when he stopped caring about them. He learned its grammar the way he'd learned every interface that had ever been put in front of him: fast, by touching it, by paying attention to what it rewarded. A fallen branch, when he looked at it, offered up a faint outline and a single word — **Branch (improvised)** — and a number, *3,* which he understood to be how badly it could hurt something. A cluster of pale mushrooms reported **Unknown — sample to identify.** The moss reported nothing, which he was obscurely grateful for. Not everything wanted to be useful. Some things were allowed to just be moss.

This was, he realized, exactly the part he was good at. The part the recruiters had measured. The world had handed him chaos and the implant had handed him *rules,* and between the two of them was the narrow bright country where Marcus Vye had always lived — the place where a frightening thing becomes a solvable one, where panic gets quietly converted into a list. *Take the branch. Stay off the open ground. Find water by going downhill. Do not eat the mushrooms.* He could survive a list. He had been surviving lists his whole life. The trembling in his hands began, fractionally, to ease.

Which is when he understood the trap of it, all at once, with the cold clean clarity that the implant could not give him because it was the one thing the implant did not want him to feel.

The interface was not showing him the world.

It was standing *between* him and the world — a translator, a merciful liar, taking something too large and too old and too indifferent to be looked at directly and rendering it down into branches with damage values and bars that emptied politely. And a translator chooses its words. A translator decides what you are allowed to understand. He was not seeing the Realm. He was seeing the Realm's *patience* with him, and patience was a thing that could end.

*The deeper you go,* something in him said, in a voice that was not the interface and was not quite his own, *the less it will bother to pretend.*

---

He did not hear the woman arrive. He felt the interface react to her — a new bar resolving at the edge of his vision, green where his was red, and beside it a name his eyes refused to read at first, the letters swimming, the way the truly important things kept arriving smaller and harder than the rest.

She came out of the treeline at a dead, controlled run, low and quiet, a blade already drawn and held back along her forearm the way you carry steel when you have used it before and expect to again. Mud to the knee. A burn, half-healed, climbing one side of her jaw. And her eyes — her eyes found him and *catalogued* him in under a second, the way his own attention catalogued a fallen branch, and he had the disorienting experience of watching someone do to him exactly what he had spent the last hour doing to the forest.

"You're new," she said. Not a question. Her gaze flicked up, above his head, to the thing he couldn't see from the inside — his own bar, his own absurd glowing **Lv. 1** — and something in her face closed like a door. "God. You're *brand* new. You don't even have a class yet."

"I don't," Marcus agreed carefully, "have any idea what that means."

"It means run when I tell you to." She wasn't looking at him anymore. She was looking back the way she'd come, at the trees, at the long tired bars of light, and the light was different now — he saw it the instant she did, saw the interface struggle to find a number for the thing moving through the canopy and *fail,* the reticle stuttering, the helpful little outline flickering on and off around a shape that was simply too large to be a branch with a damage value of three.

For the first time since he'd woken, the world had no word for what was coming.

The woman's mouth tightened. "Of course," she said, to no one, to the sky, with the bone-deep weariness of someone who has been failed by this exact thing before. "Of *course* it sends me a tutorial."

Then she grabbed his wrist, and the number above her head — *Serra,* his eyes finally consented to read, **Serra Hawthorne** — flared as she moved, and she said the only word the Realm had bothered to make him understand:

"*Run.*"
