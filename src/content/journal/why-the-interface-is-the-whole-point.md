When people hear "a gamer wakes up in a fantasy world and sees health bars,"
they assume the bars are the hook — a bit of nostalgia, a wink at the reader, a
shortcut past the hard work of describing pain and danger. For a while I worried
they were right, and that I'd built a novel on a gimmick.

Then I understood what the interface actually *is*, and the whole book turned
over in my hands.

The interface is not a feature of the Realm. It is Marcus's. It is the only thing
standing between a human mind and a world that was never meant to be perceived by
one — a layer of comforting arithmetic laid over something vast and indifferent
and old. Health bars don't trivialize the danger. They are how he survives
looking at it. The number is a mercy. The number is also a lie.

So every health bar in this book is a small tragedy waiting to be noticed. The
reader and Marcus see the same green glow over the same corpse, the same "Locked
(Lv. 12)" on the same door — and the story is the slow, frightening process of
learning what the glow leaves out. The abstraction frays as he descends, and the
fraying *is the plot*. Not the magic system. The cost of needing one.

That's the part I never want to lose sight of while drafting: the interface was
never the gimmick. The interface is the grief.

— The Author
