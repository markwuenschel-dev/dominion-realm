  A craft note on keeping the Realm bigger than its system — and writing prose
  that knows more than its narrator does.

The hardest sentences in this book are the ones that have to know more than
Soren does.

He reads the Realm through an interface that flattens it into something
playable — tiers, stats, the six ocular stages of the Eyes laid out like a skill
tree. That's his lens, and on the page I have to honor it: when he looks at a
threat, the prose reaches for the lattice's vocabulary, because that's genuinely
how he experiences the moment. But the *world* doesn't obey that vocabulary, and
the writing has to quietly let the reader feel the seams the narrator can't.

In practice that means writing two things at once. There's the legible
surface — the number, the label, the clean RPG logic Soren leans on. And under
it, in the rhythm and the imagery and the things described a half-beat too
vividly, there's the substrate: a world that is older and stranger than any
system can hold, and that occasionally presses through the abstraction like a
hand against thin cloth.

I keep a private rule while drafting: the map is allowed to be wrong, but it is
never allowed to be *small*. Every time the interface gives Soren a tidy answer,
the surrounding prose has to leave a door ajar — a detail the number didn't
account for, a warmth in the wood, a color the glow has no name for. The system
is a comfort. The book's job is to keep the world too large for it.

Some days that balance is the whole craft of the thing. Most days, honestly, it
just feels like trying to describe a sound through a wall and trusting you'll
hear it too.
